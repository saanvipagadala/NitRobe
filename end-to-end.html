NitRobe, the full story

The whole project in the order it was worked out: the problem, why nobody has solved it, the invention, what was built, and where it is weakest. The short version is on the overview.

The problem

Nitrous oxide is the third worst greenhouse gas and the biggest destroyer of the ozone layer. 70% of it comes out of farm soil. Only 1 enzyme on Earth can destroy it.

Every spring, farmers coat the seed of soybean and other legumes with bags of live bacteria. Most of those bacteria cannot make that enzyme. This is one of the very few places on Earth where humans choose which microbes live in the ground.

Choose wisely, and a significant amount of greenhouse gas emission can be cut.

The whole idea

Before sowing, legume farmers coat their seed with live bacteria. The bacteria move into the roots, live there all season, and feed the plant nitrogen.

A company chooses which bacteria go into each bag. It chooses the ones that race into the roots fastest, because a strain that loses that race never gets into the plant at all. Nobody has ever chosen for anything else.

Most of the fast ones cannot destroy nitrous oxide. Soybeans grown with a strain that cannot destroy it released 18.4 times more of the gas than soybeans grown with a strain that can.

For 13 years, scientists have taken strains that destroy the gas and tried to teach them to win that race. They keep losing it.

NitRobe goes the other way. Take the strain that already wins, and give it the genes it is missing. Then the bacteria farmers already buy would destroy the gas instead of letting it go.

Everything below is the argument for that, written as questions. Open the ones you want.

From fertilizer to greenhouse gas, in 4 steps

Fertilizer feeds half the people alive. So how does a good thing end up as a greenhouse gas? Here is what happens to it in the ground.

Why nitrous oxide, and not carbon dioxide?

Because 1 molecule of it traps 273 times more heat, and almost nobody has heard of it. Carbon dioxide and methane are the 2 greenhouse gases everybody knows. The 3rd goes unnamed, and per molecule it is the worst of the 3.

Gas Heat it traps, against carbon dioxide
Carbon dioxide 1
Methane 28
Nitrous oxide 273

Farmers put nitrogen fertilizer on their fields. Crops never take up all of it, and soil bacteria turn the leftovers into nitrous oxide. That is where 70% of the world's nitrous oxide comes from, and it has risen 40% since 1980.

It also destroys the ozone layer, which is what shields us from the ultraviolet light that causes skin cancer.

Using less fertilizer is not an option. Less fertilizer means less food, and the world needs the food. So the question is not how to put less nitrogen into soil. The question is what happens to the nitrogen once it is there.

What is fertilizer, exactly?

Nitrogen, packaged so a plant can eat it. The 2 common kinds look like this.

Fertilizer Formula What it is
Urea CO(NH2)2 The most used nitrogen fertilizer in the world
Ammonium nitrate NH4NO3 Ammonium and nitrate, already joined together

Both break down in wet soil into the form plants actually take up through their roots, which is nitrate, NO3-: 1 nitrogen atom carrying 3 oxygens.

What happens to the nitrate the crop does not eat?

It rains, and bacteria start breathing the leftovers. A crop never takes up all the nitrate. The rest sits in the soil until water fills the spaces between the grains and pushes the air out.

Bacteria down there still need to breathe. Breathing means passing electrons to something, and normally that something is oxygen, O2. With the oxygen gone, some bacteria switch to breathing the nitrate instead, stripping its oxygen atoms off 1 at a time.

That is the whole chain. Watch the oxygens disappear.

Step The bacterium turns into What that means
1 nitrate NO3- nitrite NO2- 1 oxygen comes off. 2 left
2 nitrite NO2- nitric oxide NO Another oxygen off. 1 left
3 nitric oxide NO nitrous oxide N2O 2 of them join up and share the last oxygen. This is where the greenhouse gas is made
4 nitrous oxide N2O nitrogen gas N2 The last oxygen comes off. This is the only way the gas is ever destroyed, and what is left is 78% of the air we breathe

Each step is run by a different enzyme, and a bacterium only performs the steps it has genes for.

That last step is performed by an enzyme, and the gene that builds that enzyme is called nosZ. A bacterium with the nosZ gene can destroy nitrous oxide. A bacterium without the nosZ gene cannot.

Taking the final oxygen off nitrous oxide is the only way nitrous oxide is destroyed by anything living, anywhere on Earth. There is no second route. From here on, the nosZ gene is what this whole project is about.

The job is done by 1 family of 7 genes, not by 1 gene. They sit side by side in the genome and are named nosR, nosZ, nosD, nosF, nosY, nosL and nosX. Only nosZ builds the enzyme itself. The other 6 deliver the copper it needs, put it in the right place and switch it on, so nosZ alone produces an enzyme that does nothing. This page calls all 7 together the nos genes.

So why does any gas escape at all?

Because soil is a community, not 1 bacterium. 1 bacterium can make the N2O and a neighbouring bacterium carrying the nos genes can finish it off, so no single cell needs all 4 steps. In most soils the gas is destroyed before it can escape.

What decides whether a field releases the gas or absorbs it is the balance between the bacteria that make N2O and the bacteria that destroy it. That balance is set by how acid the soil is, how often the soil floods, how much carbon the bacteria have to eat, and which species happen to live there.

So the gas escapes where that balance tips the wrong way. The useful question is where a human can tip the balance on purpose.

The bag, and who decides what goes in it

Nobody chooses the microbes in an acre of soil. There is 1 family of exceptions, and it is the legumes.

Why does a legume farmer buy a bag of bacteria?

Because for a legume, the bacteria are the fertilizer. The air above every field is 78% nitrogen gas, more than a crop could ever use, and no plant can touch it. The 2 nitrogen atoms are held together by the strongest bond in ordinary chemistry. That is the whole reason fertilizer factories exist: they break that bond with enormous heat and pressure.

Rhizobia break it at room temperature, and legumes have struck a deal with them.

Side of the deal What it gives
The plant A root nodule to live in, and sugar to eat
The rhizobia Nitrogen pulled straight out of the air, all season, for free

But rhizobia do not come with the seed, and each legume accepts only its own matching kind. Most of these crops are now grown far from where they evolved, so the matching bacteria never travelled with them. Plant soybean in a field that has never grown it and the plant makes no nodules, fixes no nitrogen, and goes hungry.

So the farmer buys them. Somebody chooses which strain goes in that bag, and that is the only place in agriculture where a human decides which microbes go into the ground.

Why soybean, and not corn?

Because soybean is the biggest inoculated crop, and the only one where the strain in the bag decides the outcome. 4 strains cover more than 35 million hectares in Brazil alone. The bacteria used on alfalfa, peas and clover mostly belong to species that already carry the genes. Soybean is the crop whose 2 commercial species fall on opposite sides of that line.

Corn is the bigger target and it is out of reach. Corn is a grass, grasses grow no nodules, so there is nowhere for rhizobia to live and no bag to change. Whatever turns corn's fertilizer into nitrous oxide is whatever that field already holds, and nobody chose it.

Soybean is not a small target either. In the maize and soybean rotation, the largest cropping system in North America, roughly 40% of the nitrous oxide comes out during the soybean phase, which is the phase nobody fertilizes. It comes out anyway because the nitrogen the plant fixed for itself is stored in the nodules, and when those nodules rot at the end of the season the nitrogen-rich residue feeds the bacteria that make the gas.

The source and the lever are the same object. The nodule is where the gas comes from, and the nodule is where the inoculant lives.

What is actually in the bag?

2 bacteria that look almost identical, and only 1 of them carries the genes. Soybean inoculants use 2 closely related bacteria, Bradyrhizobium japonicum and Bradyrhizobium diazoefficiens. Until 2013 scientists counted them as a single species. When the 2 were finally separated it turned out that 1 of them carries the nosZ gene and the other does not, and by then both were already being sold by the million hectares.

Strain Where it is sold Can it destroy nitrous oxide?
B. diazoefficiens USDA 110 United States, widely yes
B. diazoefficiens CPAC 7 Brazil yes
B. japonicum CPAC 15 Brazil, since the early 1990s no
B. japonicum E109 Argentina, the elite strain no

Soybeans inoculated with E109 produced 18.4 times more nitrous oxide than soybeans inoculated with USDA 110 or CPAC 7. Same crop, same conditions, and the only difference is which bacterium came out of the bag.

Why is the strain that cannot destroy the gas the one that wins?

Because winning the race into the roots and destroying the gas are 2 unrelated abilities, and only 1 of them is ever tested.

A field that has grown soybean before is already full of rhizobia left by earlier crops. A plant grows only so many nodules, and whichever bacteria get there first take them. A strain that loses that race never gets inside the plant, gives the crop no nitrogen, and the farmer has paid for nothing. So companies test 2 things: how much nitrogen a strain hands the plant, and how reliably it wins the race. Neither test asks whether it can destroy nitrous oxide.

Here is how the literature describes the 2 strains that dominate Brazil.

Strain What scientists praise it for Has the nos genes?
CPAC 15 outstanding in its competitiveness, meaning it reliably beats rivals into the roots no
CPAC 7 high efficiency in fixing nitrogen, meaning it feeds the plant well yes

The strain praised for winning is the one that cannot destroy the gas. CPAC 15 earned its place fairly, judged against tests that were never designed to notice this.

Does the problem go away if farmers stop buying the bag?

No, it gets worse. Rhizobia do not disappear at harvest. The ones a farmer adds survive in the soil and multiply season after season, so the rhizobia living in a Brazilian soybean field today are largely descendants of strains sold into that field decades ago.

Those descendants are the ones without the nosZ gene. So those fields go on releasing the gas every season, whether or not another bag is ever opened.

Why not just sell farmers a strain that already has the genes?

In the United States that is close to what already happens, and it is the right answer there. USDA 110 carries the genes and is widely sold. A farmer buying that bag needs nothing invented.

It stops working where the winner is the strain without the genes. CPAC 15 in Brazil and E109 in Argentina dominate because they beat every rival into the roots. Put a gas-destroying strain in the bag beside them and it loses that race, so it never gets inside a plant. The farmer has paid for a bacterium that is not there, the crop gets no extra nitrogen, and the gas comes out anyway. A strain that cannot get into the nodule cannot do anything from outside it.

The field is not empty either. Those soils are already full of the descendants of strains sold decades ago, and they are the ones without the gene. A new bag has to beat them too, every season, forever.

And the search did not hand over a replacement. Of the 13 species it found, the one soybean farmers actually buy is B. diazoefficiens, which is USDA 110, the strain already in the bag. The rest mostly live on other legumes. The list is a list of species that can destroy the gas, not a list of strains that can win a soybean root.

So switching the bag works exactly where the winner already carries the genes. Everywhere else the winner is the problem, and the only way to fix a winner is to change the winner.

Give the winner the genes

13 years of work has come at this problem from 1 side. NitRobe comes at it from the other.

What has everyone else tried?

5 things, and 4 of them hit the same wall. Since 2013 people have put better strains in the bag, dug strains out of local soil, picked soybean varieties that accept them, hunted for faster species, and bred a soybean that lets nothing else in. All of it is good work.

The wall is the race into the roots. The bacteria already living in the field get there first, so the strain in the bag never gets inside enough plants to matter. The 5th attempt climbs the wall by locking the locals out, and the price is a genetically modified soybean that every country has to approve.

So what does NitRobe do differently?

Do not make the good strain competitive.
Make the competitive strain good.

Everyone else starts with a strain that destroys the gas and tries to teach it to win the race into the roots. Nobody knows how to engineer winning, which is why they keep failing in the same place.

So start from the winner instead. Take the strain that already dominates the roots and give it the set of genes it is missing. That half we do know how to engineer, because every gene in the set has already been found and copied. NitRobe never has to solve the hard half. It inherits a winner.

Has anybody already done this?

No. Every paper and patent I could find starts from the losing strain and tries to make it win.

Does adding the genes slow the winner down?

No. It should speed it up.

Taking the last oxygen off nitrous oxide releases more energy than any other step in the chain, and more than breathing air does. The bacterium is not being asked to carry a burden. It is being handed a meal it has been walking past.

This is textbook electrochemistry rather than anything measured here, and it is a prediction, not a result. Whether a strain given the genes actually grows better is experiment 2.

Does a simulation agree?

Yes. Faster in 7 of the 8 situations a bacterium meets in a field, and never slower.

Where the bacterium is Growth with the genes
Inside a root nodule, which is where it lives 57% faster
A flooded field, which is when the gas is made 25% faster
Well-drained soil, plenty of air no difference

That last row is what convinced me the simulation was behaving. With plenty of air, bacteria breathe air and ignore this chain, so the genes do nothing. That is also exactly when the field is not making the gas.

All 8 situations are in scenarios.csv.

What I built, and what it found

Everything above says the strain in the bag is the wrong one. So I went looking for a better one.

Is there already a strain that destroys the gas?

Nobody knows, because nobody has ever checked. Thousands of rhizobium strains sit in university collections and company freezers. Every one was screened for feeding the plant and for winning the race into the roots. None was ever screened for destroying nitrous oxide, because nobody thought to ask.

Why not just look it up in a database?

Because the labels are wrong. The obvious way is to look each strain up in a gene database, and that does not work. The same gene is filed under different names in different genomes depending on who typed the entry, so a search for the name misses the gene. I tried it on 9 commercial strains and it got one of them flatly wrong.

The other way is to test strains on a bench, one at a time, which nobody is going to do thousands of times on a hunch.

How does the search work?

I built a model that reads a bacterium's DNA and finds the gene in it, without ever reading the name somebody filed that gene under.

I showed it 3,994 real copies of nosZ, pulled from the European Nucleotide Archive, and 11,781 things that are not it, including nirK, the gene that makes the gas one step earlier. From those it worked out which short runs of letters tell the two apart, and it now scores any piece of DNA from 0 to 1.

Scoring a short piece is not the same as finding a gene in a genome of 9 million letters. So it walks the whole genome 2,000 letters at a time, both directions, scoring every step. The highest-scoring spot is where the gene is.

Run it yourself. The gene finder does exactly this in your own browser, on a real genome, while you watch. How the model was trained opens the lid on it.

Why learn it rather than look for a match. The same gene is spelled differently in every species, so an exact search only ever finds the bacterium you copied the gene from. Learning from thousands of spellings means a copy that has drifted is still recognised.

Then I checked it where the answer was already known. B. diazoefficiens USDA 110 is the strain the gene was first described in, and its position in that genome is written down. The search was never told where to look, and it put the gene at 327,000 against an entry that says 326,883. It landed on it. A model that is confident and in the wrong place is wrong, so this check matters more than any score.

Then against the strains farmers can actually buy, where microbiology already knew the answer and the model was shown none of it.

Strain What it is The search said Literature says
B. diazoefficiens USDA 110 US commercial inoculant has it, 0.99 has it ✓
B. japonicum SEMIA 5079, better known as CPAC 15 Brazilian commercial inoculant, 30 years, millions of hectares lacks it, 0.29 lacks it ✓
B. elkanii common in soybean soils lacks it, 0.32 lacks it ✓

3 out of 3. The search independently reached the same answer as 30 years of microbiology, including on the strain Brazilian farmers actually buy. CPAC 15 is the one that matters: it has been sold across millions of hectares and it is the strain that cannot destroy the gas. Had the model said otherwise, this project would be built on sand.

What did it find?

13 species out of 40 carry the gene, and 10 of those are new. The search read 40 whole genomes of Bradyrhizobium, about 330 million letters of DNA.

What the search found How many The list
Species carrying the gene 13 run it yourself
Species without it 27 run it yourself
Pieces piled up, in the 13 90 to 1,938
Pieces piled up, in the 27 2 to 7
Anything in between none

For each of the 13 the list gives where in the genome the gene sits, which direction it runs, how long the stretch is, and how alike it is to the gene it was found with, which runs from 72% to 100%.

That gap matters. A search that was guessing would smear species across the middle. This one did not put a single species there.

Published work had identified 3 species with this gene. The search found 10 more.

And 1 of the 13 shows exactly why reading the sequence beats reading the label. B. frederickii has the gene, and the protein it makes is filed in the database under a different name entirely. A search by name would have walked straight past it.

What this actually delivers. Every sequenced rhizobium on Earth can now be checked in an afternoon, ranked by whether it can destroy this gas, with no laboratory and no vial opened. Try it on a genome.

The impossible job becomes a short list. 13 species are now worth testing on a bench, and until this ran, nobody knew which 13.

What can the gene finder not tell you?

Whether any of those 13 wins the race into the roots. That is a measured behaviour and it is not written in DNA. Crossing the 2 lists needs bench work, and that is the first experiment below.

And not every commercial strain has a public genome. Company strains are often sequenced privately and never deposited, so the search has nothing to read for them.

And DNA drifts faster than the protein it codes for. A gene can change letters without changing the protein it makes, so a very distant copy can slip past a search that matches DNA letter for letter. That is what happened with B. canariense: reading protein says it has the gene, reading DNA says it does not. It is the only species out of 40 where the 2 disagree.

How to find out whether it works

2 experiments would settle it, and both need a laboratory. Each is written out as a run sheet on the experiments page.

Does anything need to be built at all?

Maybe not, and that is the first thing to find out. The gene finder shortlisted 13 species. If one of those 13 destroys the gas and can also beat the bacteria already in the soil to the roots, then nothing gets engineered, nothing needs approval, and the answer is simply to change which strain goes in.

Run sheet The question How long
1 Find the N₂O destroyer A gene being present is not the same as a gene working 3 weeks

This is the first thing to run in this entire project. It is the cheapest, it involves no genetic modification at all, and it could end the engineering half before it starts. It is also where the gene finder is put on trial: 13 species were predicted from DNA alone, and the gas chromatograph either agrees or it does not.

And if they destroy the gas but lose the race?

Then the strain gets built, and 1 experiment tests both halves of it. This is the likelier outcome. The shortlist was read out of DNA, not chosen for competitiveness, and the strains that do win the race are the ones farmers already buy, which mostly carry no nos genes.

Run sheet The question How long
2 Build NitRobe Does the built strain destroy the gas, and does it still win the race into the roots? 4 months

The vials come first, because 1 week can end what 8 weeks of pots cannot. Then 40 pots of real field soil that already holds its own rhizobia, so the build has to compete for the roots the way it would in a field. That race is the claim this whole page rests on, and it is the only place it gets tested.

Both run sheets are written out as a lab would actually run it: the groups, why each control is there, what gets measured, and what every outcome would mean, including the ones that end the project. Each also comes with blank sheets to write the numbers on, a codebook defining every column, and an analysis whose rules were fixed before any data existed.

That analysis has already been run 6 times, on invented numbers, including runs where the experiment fails. Those dry runs are how I know the analysis says no when the answer is no: it correctly calls the build that loses the race a failure of the whole idea, and it refuses to judge any strain at all when the method itself is broken.

What neither experiment settles

Nitrous oxide coming off real soil, which is the claim this page finally rests on. Both experiments measure bacteria, one in a vial and one in a nodule. Measuring the gas itself means intact cores out of a soybean field, fertilised and flooded on the schedule a real field follows, with the gas read under a sealed lid on the days soil actually emits. That is worth doing only once experiment 2 has shown there is something worth measuring.
